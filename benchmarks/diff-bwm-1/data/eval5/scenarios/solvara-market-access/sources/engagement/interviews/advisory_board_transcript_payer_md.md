[00:02:30] James Whitfield: Understood, and we will. Can we start with the disease itself — how does GPP show up on your desk?

[00:02:41] Dr. Nadia Okoro: Rarely, which is the first thing to understand. Generalized pustular psoriasis is not plaque psoriasis with a different suffix. It's a distinct, severe, sometimes explosive condition — patients flare with widespread sterile pustules, fever, systemic inflammation. A bad flare is a hospital admission; people die of complications, sepsis, cardiorespiratory failure. Between flares some patients are quiet, some smolder. So you have this dual character — acute crisis management and then the maintenance question, which is where your product sits.

And I'll give you a practical wrinkle while we're here: coding. GPP should code to L40.1. In practice a lot of charts come to us coded L40.0 — plaque psoriasis — because the biller defaulted or the EMR favorite was set years ago. When we deny a GPP prior authorization, a meaningful chunk of the time it's actually a coding mismatch, not a clinical decision. If you're building the workflow, make somebody verify the diagnosis code before anything gets submitted. That one step pays for itself.

[00:05:58] Elaine Voss: That's very useful. On population — the commissioned epidemiology work puts US diagnosed prevalence at roughly seven and a half thousand patients. Does that square with what you see?

[00:07:10] Dr. Nadia Okoro: It's in the right neighborhood. On our book we can count our GPP patients without taking our shoes off, and if you scale that up nationally you land somewhere in the high single-digit thousands diagnosed. So seven-and-a-half thousand, with some range around it — I won't argue with the epidemiologists, that's their job. What I will argue with — and I say this in every one of these sessions — is anyone who takes the diagnosed number and calls it the market. Diagnosed is not treatable, and treatable is not "will be a candidate for your specific drug." Those are three different numbers and manufacturer decks collapse them constantly.

[00:09:45] James Whitfield: Let's take that apart then. Starting from the diagnosed pool, who falls out before you get to realistic candidates for a new subcutaneous maintenance biologic?

[00:10:20] Dr. Nadia Okoro: Several buckets. First, patients who are genuinely quiescent — one flare years ago, nothing since, no maintenance therapy at all, and honestly no appetite for a biologic. Second, patients well controlled on an existing systemic or biologic; their dermatologist is not going to rock the boat, and frankly neither would I. Third, the medically complicated — active malignancy workups, serious infections, patients where you'd hesitate to add any immunomodulator. Fourth — and people forget this one — patients who simply won't self-inject, although a good nursing program shrinks that bucket. And then a small group who are in-and-out of care entirely, no consistent prescriber, and never make it through any access process.

[00:13:40] James Whitfield: Any sense of how the treated fraction shows up in claims?

[00:14:05] Dr. Nadia Okoro: If you pull a GPP-coded cohort and ask how many have a systemic or biologic claim in the year, you'll get — I'd guess a little over half. Your claims people can tell you exactly; I'd trust their number over my recollection. It's a decent proxy for engagement with treatment, though not identical to candidacy — some of those treated patients are the well-controlled ones I just said won't switch, and some untreated ones are exactly the poorly-controlled patients who should be first in line. The buckets partially offset. I had a patient — sorry, this is a tangent, but it makes the point — a warehouse manager, mid-forties, two flares in eighteen months, second one put him in the ICU for six days. He was "untreated" in the claims sense the whole time because nobody had put him on maintenance anything. He is precisely who this product is for.

[00:21:55] Elaine Voss: So bottom line it for us. Of the diagnosed pool, what fraction are realistic candidates?

[00:22:40] Dr. Nadia Okoro: You want a single number, and I'll give you one, but hold it loosely and show your work when you publish it. My view: of the ones actually diagnosed, a bit over half, call it 55%, are realistic candidates for a new subcutaneous biologic. Could a great launch push past that over a few years? Maybe, at the margin. But if someone puts a planning number in front of you that implies every diagnosed patient — or worse, more than the diagnosed pool — walk out of the meeting. You take the diagnosed count, you apply that haircut, you do the arithmetic yourselves, and that is your addressable population. Nobody hands you that number; you have to build it.

[00:24:30] James Whitfield: On payer mix — the client's planning materials assume it skews heavily commercial. What does the GPP population actually look like across lines of business?

[00:25:05] Dr. Nadia Okoro: It skews commercial, but not heavily. The disease peaks in working-age adults, so commercial is a bit over half — but there's a real older tail, so Medicare is meaningful, low twenties as a share, and remember for this product that is Part D, the pharmacy benefit, because the patient injects at home. Medicaid is high teens — GPP does not respect income — and then a small uninsured sliver. If someone is modeling seventy percent commercial, they're modeling the psoriasis market they wish they had, not the GPP market that exists. Check it against claims; the claims will settle it.

[00:27:15] Elaine Voss: You raised benefit type. Say more — one internal memo we've seen contemplates buy-and-bill.

[00:27:40] Dr. Nadia Okoro: Then someone hasn't read the label — or the draft label. Buy-and-bill exists because a provider administers a drug in the office and bills the medical benefit for the drug plus administration. A subcutaneous product the patient self-administers at home never touches a site of care. There is nothing to buy and bill. It adjudicates on the pharmacy benefit, it goes through specialty pharmacy, and on the Medicare side it's a Part D coverage determination, not a Part B claim with a J-code. If your junior people are modeling ASP-plus-six economics, stop them today, because every downstream workflow decision — who submits the PA, what the appeal ladder looks like, how the hub operates — is different on the pharmacy benefit.

[00:30:10] James Whitfield: Let's do prior authorization outcomes. The client's deck models a very high first-pass approval rate. What's realistic for a product like this at launch?

[00:31:15] Dr. Nadia Okoro: I'll give you bands, because the honest answer is a band. In year one, for a new-to-market rare-disease biologic with a clean data package: on commercial, low-to-mid 60s first pass if the chart is clean — and "if the chart is clean" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Medicare Part D, call it mid-fifties; the coverage-determination process is fast but plans lean on their utilization criteria hard in year one. Medicaid is the tough one — heavy step edits, fee-for-service quirks, managed-care variation — you're looking at forty, give or take. Weight those by your actual mix and realistically you land in the high 50s blended, not the 80s the manufacturer decks always assume. I have literally never seen an 80% first-pass rate at launch for a specialty biologic. By year three, with policies matured? Different conversation. At launch, no.

[00:34:20] Elaine Voss: And when the first pass fails, why?

[00:34:45] Dr. Nadia Okoro: In order of frequency on my desk: step-therapy criteria not met or not documented; the coding mismatch we discussed — L40.0 on a GPP patient; missing chart notes, meaning we pend the case rather than deny it, and the clock restarts when documentation arrives; and occasionally formulary or site-of-care technicalities. Note the difference between denied and pended — your metrics people should track them separately because the remedies are completely different. A pend is a paperwork problem; a denial needs an appeal or a peer-to-peer.

[00:36:50] James Whitfield: On step therapy specifically — the analog biologic policies we've pulled require stepping through two conventional systemics first. Should Veloxa just design to that?

[00:37:20] Dr. Nadia Okoro: No, and I feel strongly about this one. Those policies were written for plaque psoriasis and stamped onto everything with an L40 code. Step therapy through two systemics makes no clinical sense for an acute pustular flare — that patient can be in the hospital. The workflow has to lead with a step-therapy exception, not accept the step. And exceptions are winnable if the package is right: label language specific to GPP, the guideline citation, documentation of the acute presentation or flare history, and the prior-therapy record. When that package is complete, my plan approves the exception more often than not. When it's a one-line "please approve," we don't. Build the exception dossier as a standard artifact, not an afterthought.

[00:41:30] Elaine Voss: Turnaround expectations — what should the workflow assume for determination timelines?

[00:42:00] Dr. Nadia Okoro: Assume what the law requires, not what a call-center rep tells your field team. A growing majority of states cap standard prior-authorization determinations by statute — seven calendar days is the common standard, seventy-two hours for expedited or urgent. Plans miss those windows more than anyone admits, so build your escalation logic around the statutory clock: if a statute state blows past day seven, that's a compliance conversation with the plan, not a shrug. And use the expedited lane properly — an acute GPP presentation is exactly what urgent review exists for. Prescribers underuse it because the attestation feels like work.

[00:45:10] James Whitfield: Last area — affordability. Bridge programs, copay support, assistance for patients who can't pay.

[00:45:40] Dr. Nadia Okoro: All standard, all necessary at this price point, and one bright line. Do the bridge for patients stuck in the PA queue, do the foundation route for patients who need it, and obviously you cannot put a Medicare or Medicaid patient on a copay card — that is a federal problem. Your counsel will say it more formally, but I watch manufacturers' hubs get this wrong in the field and it is the fastest way to turn a launch into a legal matter. Commercial patients, copay support, fine. Government-insured patients, independent foundation or the manufacturer's free-drug program, full stop. Also — watch accumulators on the commercial side; more of my competitor plans run them every year and they change what your copay dollars actually accomplish for the patient.

[00:49:20] Elaine Voss: If you were advising Veloxa directly, what's the one thing you'd tell them?

[00:49:45] Dr. Nadia Okoro: Respect the smallness. Four thousand-ish realistic candidates — you'll compute your own number — means every single patient's PA journey matters, and it means budget impact is genuinely modest, which is your best argument with people like me. Come to plans with honest epidemiology, a real exception pathway, clean coding, complete charts. The plans will still make you work for it — that's the job — but a rare-disease launch that runs a disciplined workflow beats a big-market launch playbook every time.
