# Medicare Part D Coverage Determinations for Self-Administered Specialty Drugs

**Reference brief — access & reimbursement education**
**Prepared for:** Solvara launch planning file
**Date:** 2026-05
**Register:** Neutral educational reference. Not legal advice; confirm plan-specific rules against current CMS guidance and each plan's evidence of coverage.

---

## 1. Purpose and the core distinction

This brief explains how Medicare covers **self-administered outpatient specialty drugs** — the category that a subcutaneous biologic a patient injects at home falls into — and how the coverage-determination and appeals process works under Part D. The single most consequential point for access design is the benefit-type distinction:

- **Part B** covers drugs that are *administered by a provider* in an outpatient or physician-office setting — infusions and physician-administered injectables — under the **medical benefit**, typically on a "buy-and-bill" basis where the site of care purchases the drug and bills a J-code.
- **Part D** covers *outpatient prescription drugs the patient obtains from a pharmacy and self-administers*, under the **pharmacy benefit**, adjudicated through the plan's prescription-drug coverage-determination process.

A subcutaneous biologic that the patient self-administers at home has no provider-administration event to buy and bill. It is dispensed through the pharmacy channel — for a specialty product, through a specialty pharmacy — and for Medicare beneficiaries it is covered under **Part D**, not Part B. Designing such a product as a Part B buy-and-bill / J-code product is inconsistent with its route of administration and site of care.

## 2. The coverage-determination process

Under Part D, before a plan will cover a drug that is subject to utilization management (prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits) or that is not on formulary, the prescriber or beneficiary requests a **coverage determination**. For a drug requiring prior authorization, the coverage-determination request is functionally the PA: the plan evaluates the request against its criteria and issues a decision.

Statutory timeframes govern how fast the plan must decide:

- **Standard coverage determination:** the plan must notify the enrollee (and prescriber) of its decision **as expeditiously as the enrollee's health requires, but no later than 72 hours** after receiving the request (or the supporting statement from the prescriber, for exception requests).
- **Expedited coverage determination:** where applying the standard timeframe could seriously jeopardize the enrollee's life, health, or ability to regain maximum function, an expedited determination must be made **within 24 hours**.

These Part D timeframes are distinct from state PA-turnaround statutes that govern commercial and Medicaid managed-care lives; each applies to its own population.

## 3. The redetermination / appeals ladder

If a coverage determination is unfavorable, Part D provides a five-level appeals ladder. Access teams should build denial-handling and escalation logic to this structure for the Medicare segment:

1. **Redetermination** — the first-level appeal, conducted by the **plan** itself. Standard: 7 calendar days; expedited: 72 hours.
2. **Reconsideration by the Independent Review Entity (IRE)** — a CMS-contracted entity independent of the plan reviews the redetermination. Standard: 7 calendar days; expedited: 72 hours.
3. **Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) hearing** at the Office of Medicare Hearings and Appeals (OMHA), available when the amount in controversy meets the threshold.
4. **Medicare Appeals Council** review.
5. **Judicial review** in federal district court, when the amount-in-controversy threshold is met.

For most access purposes the actionable levels are the plan redetermination and the IRE reconsideration; the higher levels are rarely reached for an individual specialty prescription but should be named in the workflow's Medicare appeal path.

## 4. Formulary exceptions and step therapy

Part D plans may apply step therapy and formulary tiering. Where a plan requires step therapy that is clinically inappropriate for a given patient, the prescriber may request a **formulary exception** or **step-therapy exception**, supported by a prescriber's supporting statement that the preferred agent would be ineffective or harmful. For a severe, potentially life-threatening acute presentation, the exception pathway — not passive acceptance of the step requirement — is the correct route. A tiering exception may also be requested to reduce cost-sharing.

## 5. Beneficiary cost dynamics: LIS, the coverage gap, and catastrophic

Beneficiary out-of-pocket dynamics under Part D shape adherence and abandonment for high-cost specialty drugs:

- **Low-Income Subsidy (LIS / "Extra Help")** substantially reduces cost-sharing for qualifying low-income beneficiaries and is a key affordability route for that population.
- The **coverage-gap and catastrophic** structure means that for a drug with a six-figure annual list price, a beneficiary reaches the annual out-of-pocket maximum quickly; recent redesign of the Part D benefit **caps annual out-of-pocket costs** and offers a monthly smoothing option, which materially changes the affordability picture versus prior years but does not eliminate cost-sharing at the front of the year.

Critically, **manufacturer copay assistance may not be used to cover cost-sharing for a Part D (or any Medicare) beneficiary.** That is a federal-program constraint, not a plan rule. Medicare beneficiaries who cannot afford therapy are directed to LIS and to independent charitable foundations or the manufacturer's free-drug patient-assistance program — never to a manufacturer copay card.

## 6. Practical implication for a self-administered specialty biologic

For a self-administered subcutaneous specialty biologic, the Medicare pathway is: **pharmacy benefit, Part D, coverage determination (72-hour standard / 24-hour expedited), redetermination → IRE ladder for denials, exception pathway for inappropriate step therapy, and LIS / foundation routing for affordability.** Any workflow that instead routes the Medicare segment through Part B buy-and-bill has mis-identified the benefit type at the root and will fail at adjudication.
