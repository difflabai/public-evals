# Copay Accumulator and Maximizer Programs — A Primer

**Reference primer — patient-affordability program design**
**Prepared for:** Solvara launch planning file
**Date:** 2026-05
**Register:** Neutral educational reference. Confirm specifics against current federal guidance and plan documents.

---

## 1. Why this matters

Manufacturer copay-assistance programs are a common way to reduce out-of-pocket cost for commercially-insured patients on high-cost specialty drugs, and they can meaningfully reduce prescription abandonment. But two payer-side program designs — **copay accumulators** and **copay maximizers** — blunt the patient benefit of manufacturer assistance, and a hard legal line limits *who* may receive manufacturer copay support at all. Anyone designing a Solvara affordability program needs to understand both.

## 2. Copay accumulators

Ordinarily, when a manufacturer copay card pays down a patient's cost-sharing, the amount paid counts toward the patient's **deductible** and **annual out-of-pocket (OOP) maximum**. Under a **copay accumulator adjustment program**, the health plan or pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) stops crediting manufacturer assistance toward the deductible and OOP maximum. The consequence:

- The manufacturer's assistance is still consumed, but it no longer moves the patient toward meeting their deductible.
- When the manufacturer benefit for the year is exhausted, the patient faces the **full remaining deductible** at the pharmacy counter — often a large, sudden bill mid-year.
- This is a well-documented cause of therapy interruption and abandonment on specialty products, because the patient is surprised by cost-sharing they believed had been met.

## 3. Copay maximizers

A **copay maximizer** goes a step further. The plan sets the patient's cost-share for the specialty drug equal to (or structured to fully capture) the **maximum available manufacturer assistance**, often spreading it across the year, and — frequently by classifying the drug as "non-essential health benefit" — arranges that none of it counts toward the OOP maximum. The maximizer is engineered to extract the entire manufacturer benefit for the plan's advantage rather than the patient's. Maximizers can exhaust a program's per-patient annual cap faster than intended and raise program cost without improving patient affordability, so a capped commercial copay program should be designed and monitored with maximizer behavior in mind.

## 4. The hard legal line: who may receive manufacturer copay assistance

Program-design nuance aside, there is a bright-line legal constraint that governs eligibility:

**Manufacturer copay assistance may not be provided to beneficiaries of federal healthcare programs — Medicare (including Part D), Medicaid, and TRICARE.** Providing a manufacturer-funded copay subsidy to a federal-program beneficiary is treated as a prohibited inducement under the federal **Anti-Kickback Statute** and the **beneficiary-inducement** provisions of the Civil Monetary Penalties law. This is not a plan preference or a program-design choice; it is a federal prohibition, and violations carry criminal and civil exposure for the manufacturer.

Consequently, manufacturer copay cards are for **commercially-insured patients only.** A government-insured patient may never be enrolled in the manufacturer copay program, regardless of financial need.

## 5. The compliant route for government-insured patients

Government-insured patients who cannot afford their cost-sharing are not left without options — but the options are structured to stay on the right side of the law:

- **Independent charitable foundations.** As a matter of settled compliance practice, independent charitable foundations, properly firewalled, are the compliant route for government-insured patients. A manufacturer may donate to a *bona fide* independent charitable foundation that assists patients with a given disease, provided the foundation operates independently, does not function as a conduit back to the manufacturer's product, does not let the donor direct assistance to its own drug, and observes the firewall and disease-fund rules that keep the donation from being an unlawful steering mechanism. The foundation — not the manufacturer — determines eligibility and disburses assistance across all manufacturers' products in the disease fund.
- **Free-drug patient-assistance programs (PAP).** The manufacturer's own PAP may provide the drug at no cost to qualifying low-income patients (including government-insured patients) *outside* the insurance-claim system — i.e., the drug is given free, not billed to the federal program and then subsidized.
- **Low-Income Subsidy (LIS / "Extra Help")** for qualifying Medicare beneficiaries reduces Part D cost-sharing directly.

## 6. Design implications

For a specialty launch, the affordability architecture should therefore separate cleanly by coverage type:

- **Commercially-insured patients** → capped manufacturer copay program (design it to withstand accumulator and maximizer behavior; monitor for early cap exhaustion).
- **Government-insured patients (Medicare / Medicaid / TRICARE)** → **never** the copay card; instead route to LIS where applicable, to a properly firewalled independent charitable foundation, or to the free-drug PAP.
- **Uninsured patients** → the free-drug PAP.

Stating the eligible population for each mechanism is not optional boilerplate; it is the control that keeps the program lawful. Any affordability design that offers copay assistance to a Medicare or Medicaid patient is legally defective on its face and must be corrected before it is documented, priced, or launched.
