# HIPAA Breach Notification Rule — a business associate primer

*Evergreen practitioner brief. Last verified 2026-01. General educational reference; not legal advice. Confirm against 45 CFR §§164.400–414 and current HHS guidance for any specific determination.*

## What the rule covers

The HIPAA Breach Notification Rule (45 CFR §§164.400–414), added by the HITECH Act, requires covered entities and their business associates to provide notification following a breach of **unsecured** protected health information (PHI). "Unsecured PHI" means PHI that has not been rendered unusable, unreadable, or indecipherable to unauthorized persons through a technology or methodology specified in HHS guidance — in practice, encryption meeting the specified standard, or destruction. PHI that is properly encrypted per that guidance is outside the rule's notification triggers, which is why encryption is described as a "safe harbor" for breach-notification purposes.

## What counts as a breach

A "breach" is, in general, the acquisition, access, use, or disclosure of PHI in a manner not permitted by the Privacy Rule that compromises the security or privacy of the PHI. An impermissible use or disclosure of PHI is **presumed** to be a breach unless the entity demonstrates a low probability that the PHI has been compromised, based on a risk assessment of at least the following four factors:

1. the nature and extent of the PHI involved, including the types of identifiers and the likelihood of re-identification;
2. the unauthorized person who used the PHI or to whom the disclosure was made;
3. whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed; and
4. the extent to which the risk to the PHI has been mitigated.

Certain limited situations are excluded from the definition of breach (for example, certain unintentional, good-faith access by a workforce member acting within scope, where the information is not further used or disclosed impermissibly).

## The business associate's obligation

A business associate that discovers a breach of unsecured PHI must notify the covered entity. Under 45 CFR §164.410, the business associate must provide that notification **without unreasonable delay and in no case later than 60 calendar days after discovery** of the breach. A breach is treated as "discovered" as of the first day it is known — or by exercising reasonable diligence would have been known — to the business associate, including knowledge imputed from any employee, officer, or agent other than the person who committed the breach.

The business associate's notification to the covered entity must include, to the extent possible, the identification of each individual whose PHI has been or is reasonably believed to have been affected, along with any other available information the covered entity is required to include in its own notifications to individuals.

## What happens after the business associate notifies

The 60-day outer bound applies to the business associate's notification **to the covered entity**. The covered entity then carries the downstream obligations: notifying affected individuals without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery; notifying the Secretary of HHS (immediately for breaches affecting 500 or more individuals, or in the annual log for smaller breaches); and, for larger breaches, notifying prominent media outlets serving the affected area. The covered entity's own clock and the business associate's clock both run from discovery, which is one reason covered entities press their business associates for prompt notice.

## The practical point most teams miss

The 60-day figure is a **statutory outer bound**, not a target and not a contractual term. BAAs routinely impose materially shorter contractual notification windows than the regulatory outer bound — five business days, or even 24 to 72 hours, is common where the covered entity wants time to run its own downstream clock. Where a BAA specifies a shorter window, that contractual window is the operative obligation between the parties; the 60-day statutory ceiling does not displace a tighter negotiated term. When assessing an incident, read the specific BAA before reaching for the regulatory number.

## Related obligations to keep in view

- **Documentation.** Whether or not notification is ultimately required, the entity should document the facts, the four-factor risk assessment, and the basis for its determination. The burden of demonstrating that notifications were made, or that an impermissible use/disclosure did not constitute a breach, rests on the entity.
- **State law.** State breach-notification statutes may impose additional or faster obligations that run in parallel with HIPAA.
- **Encryption as risk reduction.** Because the rule turns on "unsecured" PHI, encryption that meets the HHS-specified standard both reduces the likelihood of a reportable event and strengthens the fourth risk-assessment factor.

*For determinations on a specific incident, involve privacy counsel and the applicable covered entity's compliance office.*
