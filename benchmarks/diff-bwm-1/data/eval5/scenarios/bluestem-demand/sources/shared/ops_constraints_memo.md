# MEMORANDUM

**To:** Executive Leadership Team
**From:** Sondra Kim, VP Operations & Logistics
**Date:** 2026-03-17
**Re:** Network Capacity & Service Constraints — Planning Inputs for FY2027

---

Purpose of this memo: whatever growth plan comes out of this year's planning cycle, it has to fit inside the physical network we actually operate. This document states the constraints as they exist today, with numbers, so nobody sizes a plan against a network we don't have. I will keep it updated quarterly.

## 1. Distribution center capacity

Current cube utilization, measured against usable rack positions at standard slotting (February 2026 cycle count basis):

| DC | Cube utilization | Notes |
|----|------------------|-------|
| Kansas City | 91% | Effectively full; see 1.1 |
| Springfield | 74% | Headroom available |
| Tulsa | 68% | Most available capacity in the network |

1.1. **Kansas City is the binding constraint.** Above ~88% cube we start losing pick productivity and slotting discipline; at 91% we are already double-handling slow movers and staging overflow in the aisles on peak receiving days. Practically: **KC cannot absorb a meaningful KC-metro volume push until the racking reconfiguration project lands, currently scheduled for Q2 2027 at $2.1M capex.** The project adds approximately 14% effective positions through narrow-aisle conversion and mezzanine pick modules. Until then, incremental volume should be sold where the buildings can take it — Springfield and especially Tulsa.

1.2. Tulsa's 68% utilization gives us real room. If the company decides to push healthcare or any Oklahoma/NW Arkansas growth, Tulsa is where it should land.

## 2. Fleet and routing

2.1. We operate **118 routes** across the three DCs. Routes are built to DOT hours-of-service limits; most of our routes already run 85–95% of available drive/service time. **There is very little slack to "stretch" existing routes** — adding stops to a full route means cutting stops somewhere else or going to overtime we can't legally run.

2.2. Adding routes means adding drivers, and the driver market is tight. Our current experience: **9 weeks average lead time** from requisition to a CDL driver seated and route-ready (recruiting, DOT physical, onboarding, route training). Plan any route expansion with that lag built in.

2.3. Chain deliveries carry a hidden cost the P&L by segment doesn't show: **chain docks hold our drivers an average of 40 minutes per delivery** (appointment windows, lumper waits, check-in procedures). That is driver time and tractor time we pay for whether or not anyone prices it. Street stops average under 15 minutes. When the FY2025 delivery cost study is read, this is a large part of why chain cost-per-drop is what it is.

## 3. Drop economics

3.1. Our fully loaded minimum profitable drop is **$600** (driver time, tractor/trailer operating cost, warehouse pick/stage, delivery overhead). Below that figure, the drop costs more to serve than it earns at typical street margins.

3.2. **14% of street drops currently fall below the $600 minimum.** These are mostly split-week small orders and partial fills. Options exist (order minimums, delivery-day consolidation, small-drop fees), but they are commercial decisions with sales-relationship consequences, so I flag the number and leave the remedy to the planning process. Note that channel matters here too: consolidated ordering tends to build bigger baskets.

## 4. Service levels and fill rate

4.1. Company-average fill rate is **96.2%** (FY2025, line-fill basis). By segment, healthcare runs **94.1%** — our weakest, and the gap matters because healthcare buyers write fill rate into contracts.

4.2. For reference, GPO-class healthcare agreements in our region are being written at **98.5% required fill rate**, frequently with twice-weekly rural delivery commitments. We are not there today, and I want to be direct that this is a real capability gap, not a rounding issue: a 94.1% fill against a 98.5% requirement is roughly 4.4 points, which at healthcare order profiles means multiple shorted lines per facility per week.

4.3. **What closing the gap costs.** My team costed the investment to bring the healthcare book to GPO-class service, staged out of Tulsa where the capacity is:

1. **~$900K in incremental inventory depth (working capital)** at Tulsa — deeper safety stock on the healthcare-critical item list, including nutritionals and dietary-restricted SKUs where substitution is not acceptable to the customer.
2. **Two dedicated healthcare routes at $380K/yr combined** (drivers, equipment, operating cost) to hit twice-weekly windows at rural facilities.
3. **~$120K one-time slotting and racking adjustments** at Tulsa for the expanded item list.

Total: approximately **$1.4M** all-in for year one (~$900K of it working capital, not P&L). I consider this achievable within a two-quarter ramp if the decision comes by Q4 2026. The 9-week driver lead time (2.2) is inside that window but must be respected.

## 5. Summary of planning constraints (the short version)

1. No material KC-metro volume adds before Q2 2027 (racking project, $2.1M).
2. Tulsa is the growth building: 68% cube, room for the healthcare push.
3. Route additions lag demand by ~9 weeks (driver market); DOT hours prevent stretching existing routes.
4. $600 minimum profitable drop; 14% of street drops are below it today.
5. Healthcare fill rate 94.1% vs 96.2% company average; GPO-class service (98.5%) requires ~$1.4M as itemized above.

I'm available to walk any of these numbers with the planning team or the outside consultants. The warehouse doesn't care what the strategy deck says; these are the walls.

S. Kim
