# Midwest Distributors Council
## 2025 Broadline Distribution Benchmarking Study

**Published:** November 2025
**Reporting period:** Member fiscal year 2024 (FY2024)
**Lead author:** Karen Osweiler, Research Director, Midwest Distributors Council
**Contributing analysts:** D. Pham, R. Lindqvist
**Distribution:** MDC member firms and sponsoring associations. Not for public release.

---

### 1. About this study

The Midwest Distributors Council has published its Broadline Distribution Benchmarking Study annually since 2009. The 2025 edition compiles operating and commercial metrics from 93 participating distribution companies across 14 states, collected via a standardized questionnaire fielded between March and July 2025 and supplemented with follow-up interviews with 31 participating firms.

As in prior years, we caution readers against treating any single figure in this report as a target. Benchmarks describe the distribution of outcomes among participating firms; they do not establish what is achievable or advisable for any specific company, whose customer mix, geography, and asset base may differ materially from the cohort median. Where sample sizes are small, we report medians rather than means and flag the limitation in the exhibit notes.

### 2. Methodology and cohort definitions

Participating companies are assigned to one of four cohorts based on primary channel and trailing-twelve-month revenue. Cohort assignment is made by MDC research staff, not self-selected, and is reviewed annually. The 2025 cohorts are:

- **Regional broadline distributors ($250M-$1B revenue).** Full-line foodservice distributors serving restaurants, education, healthcare, and institutional accounts within a multi-state but sub-national footprint. n = 41. This is the study's core cohort and the basis for most exhibits.
- **National broadline distributors (>$5B revenue).** Full-line distributors with national or near-national coverage. n = 6. Given the small sample, figures for this cohort should be read as indicative only.
- **Convenience-channel distributors.** Distributors whose primary customer base is convenience stores, fuel retail, and micro-markets. Product mix skews to packaged goods, candy, and tobacco rather than foodservice case goods. n = 19. **Metrics for this cohort are not comparable to broadline foodservice benchmarks and should not be used as a peer reference for broadline distributors.**
- **Specialty and produce distributors.** Single-category or short-line houses (produce, protein, dairy, bakery). n = 27.

Revenue cohort boundaries are applied to FY2024 reported revenue. A regional broadline distributor reporting, for example, $480M in revenue falls squarely within the $250M-$1B cohort.

All private-label figures are reported as a percentage of gross sales, including private-label programs operated under licensed or co-operative brand marks. Digital ordering figures count unique active customer accounts that placed at least one order through a distributor-operated digital channel (web portal, mobile application, or EDI-lite storefront) during FY2024.

### 3. Exhibit 4 — Private-label penetration by cohort

Median private-label penetration among regional broadline distributors in the $250M-$1B revenue cohort was 19.6% of sales in FY2024, with an interquartile range of 18-22%. This continues the steady climb we have observed since 2019 (FY2019 median: 14.2%), driven primarily by penetration gains in disposables, chemicals, and center-of-plate proteins rather than by new category launches.

| Cohort | n | Median PL penetration (% of sales) | Interquartile range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regional broadline distributors ($250M-$1B) | 41 | 19.6% | 18-22% |
| National broadline distributors (>$5B) | 6 | 34.1% | 31-37% |
| Convenience-channel distributors | 19 | 10.4% | 8-13% |
| Specialty and produce distributors | 27 | 6.8% | 5-9% |

*Exhibit 4 notes:* The convenience-channel figure (10.4%) reflects a fundamentally different product mix — heavily weighted to branded packaged goods and tobacco, where private-label economics are structurally weaker — and is not a valid comparison point for broadline foodservice operators. The national broadliner figure benefits from owned manufacturing and import programs that are generally unavailable at regional scale; regional firms in the top quartile of PL penetration reached 22-24% without owned manufacturing, typically through co-operative sourcing programs.

Participating regional firms attribute PL gains to three practices, in descending order of citation frequency: (1) rep incentives tied to PL lines carried per account, (2) cutting-and-sampling programs at the account level, and (3) closing category gaps in disposables and chemicals, where switching resistance is lowest.

### 4. Exhibit 7 — Digital ordering adoption

Digital ordering continued its post-2020 climb. Among regional broadline distributors, the median firm reported that 65% of its independent (street) restaurant accounts transacted through a digital channel in FY2024; the top quartile reported 74% or higher. Adoption among institutional accounts (education, healthcare) is higher still, driven by procurement-system integration.

| Metric — regional broadline cohort | Median | Top quartile |
|---|---|---|
| Independent accounts transacting digitally (% of accounts) | 65% | 74% |
| Share of independent-account order lines entered digitally | 58% | 71% |
| Reported order-entry error rate, digital vs. rep-entered | roughly 1/3 of rep-entered | — |

Firms in the top adoption quartile consistently reported two enabling conditions: a sales compensation plan that is neutral or positive toward digitally placed orders, and a rep role redefined around category advice rather than order entry. Firms reporting stalled adoption most often cited rep resistance where digital orders reduced commissionable credit. We note the direction of causality here is difficult to establish from survey data alone.

### 5. Market growth context

Drawing on member shipment data and third-party channel trackers, we estimate the following FY2024 growth rates for the West North Central region, which we expect to persist at similar rates through 2026:

- **Regional healthcare foodservice:** +4.8% per year (distributed volume, nominal), supported by senior-living census growth and the continued outsourcing of hospital foodservice.
- **Independent restaurant case volume:** +2.1% per year. Independent unit counts in the region were roughly flat; growth came from per-unit volume.
- **K-12 and college dining:** +1.9% per year, largely enrollment- and reimbursement-driven, with pricing capped by fixed-price bid structures.

Readers should resist applying national restaurant-industry growth projections to regional independent volume; national figures are dominated by chain expansion and coastal price inflation, and have exceeded regional independent case growth in every year of this study's history.

### 6. Caveats and use

Participation is voluntary and the sample, while stable year over year, is not random; firms in financial distress are likely under-represented. Cohort medians can mask wide within-cohort variation. Metric definitions occasionally differ from firms' internal definitions — in particular, several participants noted that internal CRM "lapse" flags run well above true annual account attrition; our attrition figures use a 12-month no-order definition. Questions on methodology or cohort assignment may be directed to the research office (research@mdcouncil.example).
