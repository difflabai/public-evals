Dear Evan,

Thank you for the time you and your team spent with us over the past few weeks. This letter confirms our decision to retain Harrow & Vance and sets down, in my own words, what I want out of this engagement — so that neither of us can pretend later that the question was something else.

Here is the question: **How does Bluestem grow demand for its products and services without destroying margin?**

That is the whole assignment. Not a reorganization, not a technology roadmap, not an acquisition hunt. Demand growth, with the margin intact.

Some context, because you will hear a lot of numbers in the next six weeks and I want you to have mine first. My father, Earl Weyandt, started this company in 1968 with one truck and a rented dock. We are still family-owned, and I intend to keep it that way. Today we run three distribution centers — Kansas City, Springfield, and Tulsa — and we finished FY2025 at $481.2M in revenue across roughly 2,600 active accounts: independent restaurants, schools and college dining, healthcare kitchens, regional chains, our Bluestem private brands, and a modest equipment and supplies business.

On paper, that top line looks healthy. We have grown revenue every year for the last three. But I sign the financial statements, and I can tell you the gross profit line has barely moved while the revenue line has climbed. Something in the mix of what we are selling, and who we are selling it to, is eating what growth should be putting in the bank. I have my suspicions. I want yours to come from the data, not from me.

You will hear competing theories inside this building, and I want them all on the table. Dale Hutmacher, who has run our sales organization for twenty-four years and has earned the right to be heard, is convinced our problem is price. Dale believes we should get more aggressive on price; I want that thesis tested, not assumed. Priya Raghunathan, my CFO, will show you numbers that point somewhere else entirely. Marcus Ellery, our controller, is the most careful person in the company with a definition — when two figures disagree, start with him. Sondra Kim will tell you exactly what our trucks and buildings can and cannot do, and she will be right.

A few ground rules from me:

1. **Gross profit is the scoreboard.** I have told my leadership team this until they are tired of hearing it: growth that doesn't show up in gross profit is just more trucks. Every recommendation you make should survive that sentence.

2. **Work within our means.** We budget roughly $6M a year in capital spending, we carry a $25M revolver, and that facility comes with a leverage covenant I have no intention of testing. We are not raising outside equity and we are not betting the company. Your plan has to be fundable out of the business we already run.

3. **Organic growth only, this phase.** Bankers send me teasers every month. Set them aside. If the right tuck-in exists, that is a conversation for next year, after we have proven we can grow the business we already own.

4. **Tell me the truth, especially about my own people.** If a number circulating in our leadership meetings is wrong, I want to know which one, why, and what the right one is. I would rather be embarrassed in a conference room in July than in a bank meeting in December.

On logistics: this is a six-week engagement beginning the first week of June, with a final readout to me and the board in mid-August 2026. Priya will be your day-to-day contact and will open the data room to your team — financial statements by segment, the CRM extract, our delivery cost study, the Springfield pricing pilot, the customer survey we fielded in March, and whatever else you ask for. You will have interview access to anyone in the company, and I encourage you to get out on the trucks and into our customers' kitchens. This business is learned at the dock, not in the boardroom.

I am asking one question, Evan. I expect one answer, with the arithmetic behind it.

We are glad to have you.

Warm regards,
