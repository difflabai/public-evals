[00:00:30] **KIM:** Three DCs. Kansas City is the flagship and it is full. Ninety-one percent cube utilization as of the spring count. Past about eighty-five percent, a warehouse stops behaving — you're double-handling pallets, slotting gets improvised, pick paths get longer, and your error rate creeps. We're tetris-ing pallets in KC every night. My night shift supervisor has a whiteboard that would make your eyes water. Springfield is at seventy-four percent, comfortable. Tulsa is at sixty-eight, and Tulsa is where the headroom lives.

[00:01:12] **INTERVIEWER:** What relieves KC?

[00:01:14] **KIM:** The racking project. We re-rack about a third of the building, go higher and tighter, new narrow-aisle equipment. It's $2.1 million of capex and it's scheduled to land Q2 2027. Until it lands, I have essentially no incremental cube in Kansas City. I want to be very precise about this because it has planning consequences: any initiative that adds KC-metro volume before mid-2027 is competing for space that does not exist. After the racking, different conversation.

[00:01:53] **INTERVIEWER:** And the fleet?

[00:01:55] **KIM:** 118 routes across the three DCs — roughly seventy out of Kansas City, the rest split between Springfield and Tulsa. The binding constraint on routes isn't tractors, it's two things: DOT hours-of-service rules and the driver market. Hours-of-service means I cannot just stretch a route — a driver has a legal clock, and when a route is built to eleven hours of drive-and-deliver, adding two stops isn't a favor, it's a violation. So new volume in a full territory means a new route. And a new route means a new driver, and right now a CDL driver takes about nine weeks to source, screen, and onboard — that's lead time, not effort. If someone wants volume live in August, the driver requisition needed to happen in June. This is the part of demand planning that sales consistently discovers in arrears.

[00:02:52] **INTERVIEWER:** Talk to me about drop economics. Your memo has a $600 figure.

[00:02:57] **KIM:** Minimum profitable drop, fully loaded — truck, driver, fuel, insurance, the works — is about $600 for us. Below that, the delivery costs more than the gross profit on the order. Right now about fourteen percent of our street drops come in under that line. Some of that is legitimate — a good account having a light week — but a chunk of it is standing twice-a-week service for accounts that should be once-a-week, or order minimums we quietly stopped enforcing years ago. I want to be careful here: the answer is not to fire small customers. The answer is drop consolidation, minimums with teeth, and honestly, the portal helps — a customer who builds their own order against a visible minimum behaves differently than one who calls a rep who'll never say no.

[00:03:52] **INTERVIEWER:** How do the segments differ on cost to serve?

[00:03:56] **KIM:** Night and day. Street is lots of small drops, short miles, in and out of a restaurant back door in ten minutes. Chain is fewer, bigger drops but long stem miles and — this is the killer — the dock. We averaged about a forty-minute hold at chain docks last year: forty minutes at a chain dock, driver's on the clock, that's the margin right there. They schedule you a window, you show up in the window, and then you sit, because their receiving crew is unloading somebody else or on break. And there's no recourse — try billing a chain for dock time and see how your next contract negotiation goes. I've got drivers who can do six street stops in the time one chain dock holds them. My team put actual numbers on all of this in the January cost-to-serve study — drops and cost per drop by segment, quarter by quarter. I'd rather you take the figures from that file than from my memory, but the shape of it is: chain looks efficient per case and is expensive per hour.

[00:05:01] **INTERVIEWER:** When finance loads that delivery cost against chain margin, the contribution gets very thin.

[00:05:07] **KIM:** That's Priya's math to present and I won't step on it, but I'll say this: nothing in her math surprised anyone who rides a chain route. My drivers call the chain runs "the long free ones."

[00:05:22] **INTERVIEWER:** Healthcare. The Heartland loss put fill rate in the spotlight. Where is it really?

[00:05:28] **KIM:** Companywide fill rate is 96.2 percent, which is respectable for broadline. The healthcare segment specifically was running 94.1 at the time of the Heartland bid, and that is what it is — healthcare orders skew toward specific therapeutic and dietary SKUs where our inventory depth was thin, so a stockout hits them harder and more often. A restaurant substitutes; a renal-diet kitchen can't. The GPO wanted 98.5 guaranteed, with twice-weekly delivery to rural facilities. We couldn't sign that honestly, and to our credit we didn't sign it dishonestly. The awarded distributor signed it.

[00:06:04] **INTERVIEWER:** Is 98.5 an unreasonable ask?

[00:06:07] **KIM:** No. It's expensive, but it's not unreasonable, and other distributors hit it for GPO business every day. The last point and a half of fill rate is the costly part — it's not about working harder, it's about carrying depth on the slow-moving tail of the SKU set, which is working capital sitting on a shelf most weeks. That's a deliberate financial decision, not an operational heroic. Which is why I costed it instead of promising it.

[00:06:52] **INTERVIEWER:** So what would it take for Bluestem to sign it honestly at the spring 2027 rebid?

[00:06:58] **KIM:** It's in the memo: about $1.4 million all-in. Roughly $900 thousand of working capital in Tulsa inventory depth — wider and deeper stock on the healthcare SKU set so we stop shorting the long tail. Two dedicated healthcare routes out of Tulsa at about $380 thousand a year combined, so rural facilities get their twice-weekly windows without competing with street stops. And about $120 thousand of slotting work to set up a proper healthcare pick zone. None of it is exotic. It's inventory, trucks, and shelf space. But it has to start well before the bid, because the GPO isn't going to buy a promise from a distributor sitting at 94 — they need to see a service record. Every quarter we wait is a quarter of track record we don't have.

[00:08:02] **INTERVIEWER:** So if the plan we bring says "grow healthcare out of Tulsa, hold KC-metro chain flat until racking" —

[00:08:09] **KIM:** Then operations will co-sign it the same day. I can absorb the healthcare push in Tulsa tomorrow. Tulsa has cube, Tulsa has dock capacity, Tulsa can hire drivers faster than KC — the Tulsa driver market is meaningfully looser. The network wants this plan. What the network cannot do is everything at once in Kansas City in FY2026.

[00:08:47] **INTERVIEWER:** Anything on the portal from an ops perspective?

[00:08:50] **KIM:** Quietly, it's one of my favorite things in the company. Portal orders come in cleaner — the error rate on rep-keyed orders is about three times the portal rate, and every keying error is a mispick, a credit, sometimes a redelivery, which is the most expensive truck we run.
